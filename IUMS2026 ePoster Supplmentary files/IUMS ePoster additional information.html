2. Methods

2.1 Site description and sample collection

Quesnel Lake is a deep freshwater, oligotrophic, fjord-type lake situated in British Columbia, Canada (Petticrew et al., 2015). The North Arm is separated from the main lake basin by a minor sill at 135 m depth and is unaffected by the Mount Polley Mine Disaster (Hatam et al., 2019). The North Arm reaches a maximum depth of 155 m shallowing northward to approximately 1020 m deep near the Mitchell River delta, with an estimated sedimentation rate of ca. 0.4 mm yr-1 (Gilbert and Desloges, 2012). Terrestrially, the North Arm is bordered by high-relief mountains (> 2000 m elevation). Since 2014, the surrounding area has experienced approximately 98 km2 of forest fires, extending in part to within less than 6 km (mean distance 2.5 km) of the shoreline (Table S1) (British Columbia Wildfire Service, 2025; Gilbert and Desloges, 2012). These surrounding coniferous forests are situated within the interior Cedar  Hemlock Biogeoclimatic Ecosystem Classification (BEC) zone of British Columbia (Forest Analysis and Inventory Branch, 2021).

In July 2023, fourteen cores were collected from the North Arm (Fig. S1; Table S2) using a piston-type gravity corer (50 cm length, 4.76 cm internal diameter), following the design of (Glew et al., 2002). The cores were collected at approximately 6065 m depth and were stored at 4 °C upon return to the shore until extrusion. Gravity cores were extruded anaerobically at 1 cm intervals using a custom-built pneumatic core extruder housed within an anaerobic glove box. Extruded sections were subsequently homogenized into depth intervals: every 2 cm of depth was pooled for the upper 10 cm, followed by 4 cm intervals down to a total core depth of 26 cm. No bleach or ethanol was used during anaerobic extrusion to prevent the potential accumulation of vapours within the anaerobic chamber that could contaminate the sediments; instead, sectioning equipment was thoroughly cleaned mechanically between sectioning intervals to minimize cross-contamination. Of fourteen collected cores, at the field site nine cores were anaerobically extruded with four being frozen at -20 °C and five refrigerated at 4 °C, one core was left unextruded and refrigerated, 4 cores were aerobically sectioned and later discarded (Table S2). From this pool of cores, three frozen and three refrigerated cores were selected qualitatively based on similar total depths and identifiable horizon lengths. The frozen cores were used for all microbial and geochemical analyses while the refrigerated cores were selected for the incubation experiment.

2.1.1 Methane Core Measurement

Methane concentrations were measured following Su et al. (2019). Briefly, three dedicated cores for methane analysis were collected using the same gravity coring procedure described above, using core tubes with predrilled at one-centimetre intervals and sealed with reinforced duct tape prior to deployment. Immediately following collection, 1.5 ml of sediment/liquid slurry was extracted from the predrilled holes and added to 3.5 ml of 10 % NaOH, to stop microbial activity and methane cycling within the sediment. Samples were sealed in 10 ml test tubes with butyl rubber stoppers. Headspace methane concentrations were determined by gas chromatography with flame ionized detection (GC-FID; SRI Instruments, CA, USA) at Lakehead University, Ontario, Canada. Sediment methane concentration was calculated from headspace concentration (Magen et al., 2014; Sturm et al., 2015) (see Supplementary Methods). Sediments from the dedicated methane cores were discarded in-field.

2.2 Geochemical analysis

            Sediment pH was measured anaerobically using an Orion 3 pH meter (ThermoFisher, USA) with a 1:2 dilution of sediment in a 0.01 M CaCl2 solution (Carter & Gregorich, 2007). Bioavailable metals were extracted using a 1.0 M HCl digestion protocol (Yu et al., 2021). Briefly, 1 g of ground oven dried sediment was digested in a shaking incubator with 20 ml of 1.0 M HCl over 4 hours. The Northern Analytical Laboratory (NALs, Prince George, BC, Canada) then measured the concentration of Al, B, Ba, Ca, Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, K, Mg, Mn, Na, Ni, P, Pb, S, U, V, Zn using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (Agilent ICP-OES 5100, Malaysia). Total, organic and inorganic carbon and total sulfur were measured at Natural Resources Canada, CanmetMINING (Ottawa, Canada) using an Elementrac CS-d analyzer (ELTRA GmbH, Germany) (See supplemental methods). Qualitative sediment color profiling was performed using a Munsell colour chart.

2.3 Microbial community processing

            DNA extractions were performed using a Qiagen Soil DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Cat. 47014) and then purified using Promegas ProNex® Size-Selective Purification Magnetic beads (Cat. NG2001). Bacterial and archaeal 16S rRNA gene copy numbers per gram of wet sediment were determined using quantitative PCR (qPCR) on a BioRad CFX Opus 384 using SSoAdvanced Universal inhibitor Tolerant SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA-CA) and synthetic gBlocks (Bott et al., 2023). Archaeal 16S rRNA gene copy numbers were determined with Arch915F_mod (5′-AATTGGCGGGGGAGCAC-3′) and Arch 1059R (5′-GCCATGCACCWCCTCT-3′), while bacteria were quantified by Bac908F_mod (5′-AACTCAAAKGAATTGACGGG-3′) and Bac1075R (5′-CACGAGCTGACGACARCC-3′) (Lever et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2017). Detailed thermocycling protocols, reaction mixtures, MIQE compliance parameters and raw data is provided in the supplemental methods (Bustin et al., 2009).

The same DNA extracts were submitted to the Integrated Microbiome Resource, Dalhousie University (Halifax, Nova Scotia, Canada), for library preparation and high-throughput, long-read sequencing using PacBios Vega platform. Full-length bacterial 16S rRNA genes were amplified using primers 27F (5-AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG-3) and 1492R (5-RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3) (Weisburg et al., 1991; Paliy et al., 2009; Buetas et al., 2024). Full-length archaeal 16S rRNA genes were amplified with Arch21Ftrim (5-TCCGGTTGATCCYGCCGG-3) and A1401R (5-CRGTGWGTRCAAGGRGCA-3) (Reysenbach et al., 2000; Comeau et al., 2011). Standard Vega chemistry and CCS/HIFI parameters were used and the library preparation was done with the SMRTbell Prep Kit 3.0.

            Raw sequence reads were processed in R (v4.5.0) using the DADA2 package (v1.36.0) to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and calculate relative abundances (Callahan et al., 2016). Dada2 parameters follow recommendations from Callahan et al., (2019). Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using RDP classifier against SILVA NR 99 v138.2 (Wang et al., 2007; Quast et al., 2013; Callahan et al., 2016). Because the SILVA database does not provide taxonomic classifications for Bathyarcheaota below the class level; ASVs classified as Bathyarcheaota, were reclassified using BLAST against Bathyarcheaota sequences from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB v226) (Camacho et al., 2009; Rinke et al., 2021). The resulting GTDB classifications were then compared to, and renamed in accordance with, the updated nomenclature proposed by (Hou et al., 2023). Sampling and sequencing depth coverage were evaluated through rarefaction analysis using the vegan package (v2.6-10) (Oksanen et al. 2025). Average reads per sample and phylum-level abundances are detailed in the Supplemental results. Sequencing data can be found at the European Nucleotide Archive (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/) under accession number PRJEB115187.

2.4 Incubations

Stable isotope probing and incubation experiments were initially established to empirically estimate potential methanogenesis and methanotrophy rates under in situ environmental conditions. However, no measurable activity was detected over the experimental timeframe, and these incubation protocols were discontinued (see Supplementary Methods).

2.5 Methane flux and rate modelling

            Substrate diffusion models were used to determine diffusive methane flux from sediments into overlying waters and estimated net methane cycling rates, based on methane concentrations measured within the sediments (section 2.1.1; see DAmbrosio and Harrison 2022). Briefly, these models are diffusion-reaction models based on Ficks first law and second law of diffusion, with an additional term accounting for estimated net methane cycling (DAmbrosio and Harrison, 2022). Both the REC (v3.1) and PROFILE (v1.0) models were applied, with model parameters summarized in the supplemental methods (Berg et al., 1998; Lettmann et al., 2012).

2.6 Statistical analysis

            All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2023). To determine significant differences in geochemical conditions across depth intervals, general linear mixed effects models were fitted using lme4 package (v1.1-37) (Bates et al., 2015) with depth interval as the fixed effect and core ID as a random effect. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using emmeans package (v2.0.4; Lenth, 2026) and the false discover rate was corrected with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. KS deviation, dispersion, overdispersion, outliers and residuals vs predicted were checked for each model using DHARMa (v0.5.0; Hartig, 2026) and conditional R2 was determined using performance (v0.19.0; Lüdecke et al., 2021). For the linear models, a full table of model specifications after simplification is provided in Table S3. Geochemical data were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.5.2) (Wickham, 2016).

For 16S rRNA gene data, ASV relative abundances were normalized into total 16S abundance by multiplying the relative abundance of each ASV within each domain by the total 16S copy number per domain, as determined by qPCR. This relatively novel normalization method has been validated across multiple environments (e.g. Lou et al. 2018, Barlow, Bogatyrev, and Ismagilov 2020, Jian et al. 2020, Williamson, Hughes, and Willis 2022, De Tomassi et al. 2023). To allow the direct comparison between bacterial and archaeal abundances, a 16S copy number correction was applied using rrnDB database, converting total 16S gene copy values to estimated microbial cellular abundances for each taxonomic group (Stoddard et al., 2015; Jian et al., 2020).

            Total estimated cellular abundances for bacteria and archaea were visualized on a logarithmic scale using ggplot2 with relative error (see Supplementary Methods). Domain ratios were calculated by comparing estimated cellular abundances and plotted using ggplot2 such that only ratios greater than one were displayed, with separate scales for bacteria-to-archaea and archaea-to-bacteria ratios. Total estimated cellular abundances were used in DivNet (v0.4.0) (Willis and Martin, 2018) to calculate alpha diversity with the Shannon index, and visualized using ggplot2. Estimated cellular relative abundance was used to calculate taxonomic beta diversity via the RPCA method (Martino et al., 2019), and results were plotted using MicrobiotaProcess (v1.24.0; Xu et al., 2023). Metabolic pathways for described taxa are based on assigned taxonomy and literature search (See table S4); however, to further describe methanogen potential metabolic function PICRUSt2-SC (v2.6.2) was used to determine which ASVs potentially possessed a methyl coenzyme M reductase gene (mcr; EC 2.8.4.1) (Wright and Langille, 2025). Log-transformed estimated cellular abundances of selected taxa and metabolic groups were plotted using ggplot2. Graphs of relative abundances from sequencing without qPCR and rrnDB transformation and graphs of methanogenic groups with only ASVs inferred to have the mcr gene by PICRUSt2-SC are available within the supplemental results (Fig. S1).

3. Results and Discussion

Regarding methanogenesis, methanogens were detected starting within the redox zone; however, populations only became considerable within the depauperate horizon. Methylotrophic methanogenesis was the dominant methanogenic pathway based on community membership within Quesnel Lake, while acetoclastic and hydrogenotrophic methanogens, though present, are detected at very low abundances (<103 ecpg) (Table S5). The dominant methanogenic lineage is the order Methanomassiliicoccales, which averages a peak of 6.36×104 ecpg (range: 8.88×1031.44×105) at 1822 cm. This order has not been fully resolved due to a lack of cultured representatives and was rarely classified at the family level (SILVA) within Quesnel Lake. The order Methanomassiliicoccales also differs in taxonomic annotation between SILVA and GTDB and when ASVs classified as Methanomassiliicoccales (SILVA) were reclassified with PICSRUSt2-SC the order of specific ASVs differed, with some now labeled as UBA10834 and RBG-16-68-12. However, when comparing the profiles of ASVs with PICRSUt2-SC inferred mcr genes, Methanomassiliicoccales (SILVA) was still the dominant methanogenic group though their total abundance dropped considerably (Fig S1).

Methanomassiliicoccales is generally found across diverse habitats from feces (Dridi et al., 2012), guts (Paul et al., 2012), wetlands (Weil et al., 2023) to lake sediments (Yang et al., 2020; Kadnikov et al., 2024); though metagenomic evidence has separated the order into two distinct clades, those associated with gastrointestinal tracts and those found in the environment (Xie et al., 2024). Methanomassiliicoccales is highly substrate-limited, and recent metagenomic evidence suggests that environmental clades, presumably those present in Quesnel Lake, are methanol-specific H2-dependent methylotrophic methanogens (Xie et al., 2024). Although theoretical pathways have been proposed, suggesting that Methanomassiliicoccales can perform acetoclastic methanogenesis, these pathways have not been confirmed metagenomically or experimentally (Speth and Orphan, 2018; Szafranek-Nakonieczna et al., 2019). It is more likely that Methanomassiliicoccales uses acetate primarily for anabolism rather than catabolism (Weil et al., 2023).

The dominance of methylotrophic methanogenesis within Quesnel Lake is unexpected; this pathway is often assumed to be the least competitive and typically becomes dominant only under unique environmental conditions that are not favourable for other methanogens, such as highly saline and sulfate-rich environments (Cozannet et al., 2020; Bueno De Mesquita et al., 2023). In freshwater systems, methylotrophic methanogen abundances are typically below 20 % of the total methanogenic community (e.g. Szafranek-Nakonieczna et al. 2019, Yakimovich et al. 2020, Meier et al. 2024). To date, only two other studies has documented the natural dominance of Methanomassiliicoccales within a freshwater lakes -Lake Pääjärvi (Rissanen et al., 2023) and Lake Joux (Bosco-Santos et al., 2026). Though, since the name Methanomassiliicoccales was only validated by the International Committee on Systematics of Prokaryotes in 2013 (Iino et al., 2013; Borrel et al., 2014), and there are mismatches within the taxonomic assignment of Methanomassiliicoccales within the NCBI, SIVLA and GTDB databases (Quast et al., 2013; Schoch et al., 2020; Rinke et al., 2021) this may represent underreporting than true novelty.

Rissanen et al. (2023) explicitly suggested that Methanomassiliicoccales dominance in Lake Pääjärvi resulted from low organic matter lability in sediments. Similarly, Bosco-Santos et al., (2026) suggests that in older refractory sediments methylotrophs gain a selective advantage over hydrogenotrophic and acetoclastic methanogens which rely on fresh labile organic matter. However, Bosco-Santos et al., (2026) interprets that methylated-substrate supply derived from algal biomass is the primary factor in Methanomassiliicoccales dominance. Integrating these perspectives, we highlight the shared terrestrial context. These three lakes are all surrounded by coniferous forests; Lake Pääjärvi is located within the northern boreal zone (Rissanen et al., 2023),  Lake Joux is surrounded fir and spruce species (Magny et al., 2008), while Quesnel Lake lies within the interior Cedar  Hemlock Bioclimatic Ecosystem Classification (BEC) Zone of British Columbia (Forest Analysis and Inventory Branch, 2021; Garris et al., 2018).

Coniferous litter is characterized by low organic matter lability, and such inputs are known to select for distinct methanogenic communities (Jílková et al., 2019; Yakimovich et al., 2020). Additionally, coniferous litter has a high lignin content which can be decomposed to methanol, by the family Baizomorphaceae within the context of Quesnel Lake (Conrad, 2020; Hou et al., 2023; Nesbø et al., 2024). Supporting the importance of coniferous litter inputs, organic carbon sedimentation in oligotrophic boreal lakes is typically dominated by allochthonous (e.g. terrestrial) sources (Gudasz et al., 2017; Thieme et al., 2019; Su et al., 2025). This is consistent with Quesnel Lake being oligotrophic and Lake Pääjärvi having an oligo-mesotrophic status as well as their locality (Petticrew et al., 2015; Rissanen et al., 2023). Alternatively, Lake Jouxs sediments contain eutrophic zones where Methanomassiliicoccales dominates; however, the pronounced eutrophication of Lake Jouxs sediments are driven my agricultural intensification and human activity within the watershed (Bosco-Santos et al., 2026). Agricultural-derived terrestrial soil erosion has been shown to increase allochthonous organic carbon inputs within eutrophic lakes, like Lake Joux, thereby providing a mechanism for coniferous litter inputs to influence methanogenic community selection (Downing et al., 2008; Gudasz et al., 2017). Although this represents third documented case of Methanomassiliicoccales dominance in freshwater lake sediments, the shared characteristics of Quesnel Lake, Lake Pääjärvi and Lake Joux suggest that interactions between coniferous litter inputs may result in this unusual methanogenic profile.

16S abundance determination by qPCR

            After extraction and purification (section 2.3), nucleic acid quantification and purity was determined on a NanoDrop ND-1000 full-spectrum UV/Vis spectrophotometer.  Each 10 µl qPCR reaction consisted of 1 µl of 1/10 diluted template, 0.5 µM of each primer (Table S7), molecular-grade water, and 1x of SSoAdvanced Universal inhibitor Tolerant SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA-CA) (Bott et al., 2023). Thermocycling conditions differed between bacterial and archaeal assays and are summarized in Table S8, but briefly, bacterial 16S quantification required a 3 step approach with separate annealing and extension step; whereas, archaeal 16S quantification was performed with a 2 step approach with a combined anneal/extension step. After the final extension step a melt curve analysis was performed between 65 and 95 °C at 0.5 °C increments for 0.05 s each to check for primer specificity. Synthetic gBlocks (IDT, Canada) containing 16S rRNA gene fragments from model organisms from each domain (Methanococcus maripaludis, 16S total archaea assay; Escherichia coli, 16S total bacteria assay) were used to generate standards curves for each assay (Table S9).

Optimal annealing temperatures for each assay was determined using gradient qPCR. Product specificity was assessed by melt curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis. Thermocycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 120 s, followed by 40 cycles of a denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 55-65 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. After the final extension step a melt curve analysis was performed from 65 and 95 °C at 0.5 °C increments (0.05 s per step) to check for primer specificity. Reaction conditions and volumes were identical to those described above. Inhibition testing was performed on 7 randomly selected samples at 1X, 1/10X, and 1/100X dilutions with 3 technical replicates each. No inhibition was detected.

All analysis was performed in CFX Maestro (v2.2; BioRad). A standard curve covered gene copy numbers from 101 to 109. The linear range and amplification efficiency differed slightly between assays (Table S10). No-template controls (NTC), and associated Cq values are reported in Table S11. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate or duplicate technical replicates if a single technical replicate had an abnormal amplification curve. Normalization was computed to starting extraction wet weight of the sediment which was determined for each sample. Raw qPCR data can be found at https://github.com/AngusBall/The-Nautilus.

Samples were sealed in 10 ml test tubes with butyl rubber stoppers and refrigerated.  When headspace methane concentrations were to be measured, sample temperature was allowed to equilibrate with room temperature and shaken to equilibrate methane concentrations between the aqueous and gas phase. Methanogenesis and Methanotrophy Incubations

Absolute rates of methanogenesis and methanotrophy were determined using sediment bag incubations amended with 13C-labeled Methane and quantified using an isotope dilution model (Xiao et al., 2017, 2018). Bag incubations were chosen to allow repeated subsampling while maintaining no headspace, thereby better approximating natural conditions. Each incubation contained 2040 g of wet sediment, depending on sample availability. Sediment was diluted with Milliq water to a final concentration of 66% sediment to allow for repeated 1 ml subsampling over the course of the experiment. Sediment slurries were transferred anaerobically into hand-fabricated incubation bags made from FoodSaver (Canada) heat-sealable bags, glass test tubes, and butyl rubber stoppers, following the design described by Xiao et al. (2017) based on (Hansen et al., 2000) (Fig. S6). Furthermore, incubations were stored in heat sealed mylar bags with an oxygen absorber to further reduce the risk of oxygen contamination of the samples. In total, each sediment section had two technical replicates and three biological replicates, for a total of 108 incubations.

For the isotope dilution model, incubations were maintained at environmentally relevant temperatures (ca. 4 °C) for one month prior to isotope addition to allow microbial communities and geochemical conditions to stabilize. Methane concentration and isotope composition were measured prior to tracer addition. Then 13C-labeled Methane was added to the incubations to achieve an ca. 2 % increase in both total methane and 13C concentration. Then incubations were subsampled for methane concentration and isotopic composition via GC-C-IRMS and GC-FID, respectively. Subsampling was conducted four times over a total incubation period of four months. Methane cycling rates were attempted to be determined using sediment bag incubations to corroborate the substrate models; however, when slurries were subsampled and sediment methane concentration was calculated as in the methane core measurements, no net change in methane concentration was detected across any technical or biological sample and the experiment was judged to be failed. This is likely due to the large delay between sample collection and incubation, which may have reduced methanogen survivability. It is possible that a longer stabilization time was necessary for the incubation; however, the inclusion of a one-month stabilization period is longer than that reported in comparable studies (e.g. Martinez-Cruz et al., 2018; Su et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2017). Finally, since oxygen diffusion into the incubations was not explicitly tested, oxygen diffusion to toxic levels for methanogenesis is theoretically possible.

Substrate models were used to determine methane flux from sediments into overlying waters and net methane cycling rates, these models are diffusion-reaction models based on Fick first law and second law of diffusion, with an additional term accounting for net methane cycling (Equation 14; 15)

            Here, J is the methane flux, dCsed/dt represents the change in methane concentration across time, t, dCsed/dz is the methane concentration gradient with sediment depth, z, ϕ is porosity, Ds is the sediment diffusion coefficient of methane in porewater, and R represents the net methane cycling rate.       

The average methane concentration measured in section 2.1.1 was assumed to represent steady-state methane concentrations. A constant sedimentation rate of 0.4 mm yr-1 was assumed based on Gilbert and Desloges (2012). Bioirrigation and bioturbation are often included in modeling calculations; however, there were no apparent burrows or macrofauna in the collected sediments; therefore, these processes were assumed to be negligible (Berg et al., 1998). For the PROFILE model, the number of unique reaction zones was determined by the model itself (Berg et al., 1998).
